What to automate first in a small business (and what to leave alone)

By Ryan Smith · November 11, 2026 · 6 min read

Most small business owners I talk to don't need "AI transformation." They need Monday morning back. Somebody on the team is copying numbers from one tab to another, retyping order details into a spreadsheet or digging through an inbox to find the inquiry that came in Saturday night. None of it is hard. It just never stops.

Automation fixes that kind of work well. But not every task is worth automating, and starting with the wrong one is how people end up paying for a system nobody uses. Here's how I decide what to automate first.

Start with a one-week time log

Before picking anything, write down every repetitive task you or your staff do for one normal week. Keep it rough: what the task is, how long it takes and how often it happens. A notes app is fine.

By Friday you'll usually see three or four tasks that show up over and over. Those are your candidates. The point isn't precision. It's getting the real list out of your head, because the task that annoys you most is not always the one that costs the most time.

The four-question test

For each task on the list, ask:

  1. Does it follow the same steps every time? If you could write the steps on an index card, it's a good fit. If every case needs judgment, it isn't (yet).
  2. Does it happen often? Something you do daily or weekly pays back fast. Something you do twice a year usually doesn't.
  3. Is the information already digital? If it lives in a form, an email, a POS system or a spreadsheet, a system can read it. If it lives on paper or in someone's memory, that comes first.
  4. What happens when it's late or wrong? Tasks where a delay costs you money (a missed inquiry, a stockout, an unpaid invoice) are worth more than tasks that are merely tedious.

A task that gets four yeses is where you start.

Good first automations

These are the ones I see pay off fastest for local businesses:

  • Inquiry and lead handling. Every inquiry lands in one place, gets sorted by whether it fits what you do, and the good ones reach you right away. This is usually the highest-value first build because slow replies lose jobs. (More on that in why slow replies cost you jobs.)
  • The weekly numbers. Sales, ad spend and email results pulled into one sheet or one email every Monday, instead of logging into five dashboards.
  • Follow-ups. Quote follow-ups, appointment reminders and review requests that go out on schedule without anyone remembering to send them.
  • Low-stock alerts. A heads-up when an item drops below a set level, before it sells out on a busy weekend.
  • Moving data between tools. New customers from your booking system into your email list, paid invoices into your bookkeeping sheet, that kind of thing.

What to leave alone

Some things look automatable and shouldn't be, at least not first:

  • Anything that depends on a relationship. Your reply to a regular customer, a tricky refund, a complaint. Automation can draft or route these, but a person should send them.
  • Processes that change every month. If you're still figuring out how you want something done, automating it locks in a version you'll want to change. Settle the process, then automate it.
  • Tasks that take five minutes a month. Not worth the setup or the upkeep.
  • Anything you can't check. If you wouldn't know the system made a mistake, don't hand it the job. Every system I build includes a way to see what it did.

Where AI fits

AI is useful for the messy middle: reading a free-text message and pulling out the date and group size, summarizing a month of reviews, drafting a reply for you to approve. It's less useful as the decision-maker. In the inquiry system I build, the rules decide what qualifies and the AI is a second set of eyes. It can flag an inquiry for review, but it can never approve one on its own. That's a good default for most small businesses: let AI read and draft, and keep the final call with a person or a clear rule.

Keep it on accounts you own

Whatever you build, make sure it runs on your own accounts (your Google Workspace, your email platform, your POS) and comes with plain instructions. If the person who built it disappears, you should still have a working system and enough notes for someone else to pick it up.

The short version

Log a week. Pick the task that repeats the most, follows the same steps and costs you money when it's late. Automate that one, make sure you can see what it's doing, and only then move to the next.
